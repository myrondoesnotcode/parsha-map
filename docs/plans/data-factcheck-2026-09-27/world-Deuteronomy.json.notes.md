## Chronology conventions and known conflicts

- Egyptian dates: conventional low chronology (Ramesses II acc. 1279, so regnal year n ≈ 1280−n: year 34 ≈ 1246, year 55 ≈ 1225).
- Hittite dates: Wikipedia is inconsistent. ‘Tudḫaliya IV’ and ‘Alaca Höyük’ give c. 1245–1215; ‘Battle of Nihriya’ gives Tudḫaliya IV c. 1237–1209 and Ḫattušili III c. 1267–1237 (short chronology); ‘Ḫattušili III’ gives 1275–1245 (middle) or 1267–1237 (short); EIEOL (UT Austin) uses older higher dates (bronze tablet ‘circa 1260-1240’). I used 1267–1237 for Ḫattušili III (short chronology, matching Muwatalli II 1295–1272 and the Egyptian low chronology) and Tudḫaliya IV’s article’s 1245–1215. A reviewer may prefer to align Tudḫaliya IV to 1237–1209; the affected events (bronze tablet, Šaušgamuwa treaty, Yazılıkaya, dams) would shift by ~8 years and still fall inside their windows except the dams (c. 1240, from the Gölpınar article, which is independent of the reign figures).
- Kaštiliašu IV / fall of Babylon: ‘Kaštiliašu IV’ gives his reign c. 1232–1225 ending in defeat, so capture c. 1225; but Wikipedia’s ‘Babylonian–Assyrian War of 1235 BCE’ dates the war ‘around 1235 BCE’. I used c. 1225 (approx). Flag.
- Ugarit hunger letter: Wikipedia ‘Ugarit’ presents it inside a letter from Merneptah (1213–1203); Archaeology magazine says Ammurapi wrote to Seti II (c. 1200–1194). The event is shown as a span 1213–1194 and the description says sources differ.
- Shalmaneser I: reign 1273–1244 or 1265–1235; events use the whole span 1273–1235.
- Anachronism check: no event is Esarhaddon (672) or Josiah (c. 622); the 7th century appears only in the notes as the date many scholars give for the writing of Deuteronomy (Wikipedia ‘Book of Deuteronomy’: “de Wette in 1805, most scholars have accepted that this portion of the book was composed in Jerusalem in the 7th century BCE in the context of religious reforms advanced by King Hezekiah (reigned c.”).

## Reuse within Deuteronomy (max twice each)

- Treaty of Muwatalli II with Alaksandu (c. 1280): vayeilech (reading clause), haazinu (witness list).
- Tudḫaliya IV–Šaušgamuwa treaty: vaetchanan (allegiance), haazinu (kings ‘equal to me’).
- Egyptian–Hittite treaty (c. 1259): ki-teitzei (refugee clause), nitzavim (binds children and grandchildren). NOTE: this treaty is also used in Exodus (mishpatim, ki-tisa) and mentioned in vayikra; kept because the refugee clause is the clearest contrast to Deut 23:16–17.
- Shalmaneser I: reeh (temple restoration), nitzavim (deportation) — different facts from his reign.
- Ramesses II and Moab: devarim (1272 campaign, Luxor statue), ki-teitzei (inscriptions of his reign naming Mw-i-bw with the Shasu of Seir).
- Nazi-Maruttash appears twice as different objects (shoftim: hemerology; ki-tavo: kudurru). Two different kudurrus: Nazi-Maruttash (ki-tavo), Kaštiliašu IV (shoftim).
- No event from Vayikra; no Exodus event other than the Egyptian–Hittite treaty.

## Per-event quotes

### devarim (window 1300–1200)

**c. 1300–1200** — A clay tablet from Ugarit, in Syria, praises Rapiu, “King of Eternity”, a god “enthroned in Ashtarat” who “rules in Edrei”.

- [Og] “A possible connection to Og and the Rephaim kings of Bashan can also be made with the much older Canaanite Ugaritic text KTU 1.108 from the 13th century B.C., which uses the term "king" in association with the root /rp/ or "Rapah" (the Rephaim of the Bible) and geographic place names that probably correspond to the cities of Ashtaroth and Edrei in the Bible, and with which king Og is expressly said to have ruled from (Deuteronomy 1:4; Joshua 9:10; 12:4; 13:12, 31).”
- [Og] “The clay tablet from Ugarit KTU 1.108 reads in whole, "May Rapiu, King of Eternity, drink [w]ine, yea, may he drink, the powerful and noble [god], the god enthroned in Ashtarat, the god who rules in Edrei, whom men hymn and honour with music on the lyre and the flute, on drum and cymbals, with castanets of ivory, among the goodly companions of Kothar.”

- [Og] “According to Matthew McAffee, the historical reminiscenses of Ugaritic mythology indicate that the Hebrew Bible has probably preserved a genuinely ancient tradition of an Amorite king Og stemming from the 2nd millennium BC.”

### ki-teitzei (window 1279–1200)

**c. 1279–1213** — Inscriptions of Ramesses II name Mw-i-bw, perhaps Moab, as a rebel land that raided Egyptian territory together with the Shasu of Mount Seir.

- [Moab] “Four inscriptions from the time of Ramesses II mention Mw-i-bw as a rebellious place that refuses to recognize Egypt's control over Canaan and, together with the Shasu of Mount Seir, conducted raids in Egypt.”
